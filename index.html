Environmental Science | Minimum four days | Fairfield, North Caldwell, Essex Fells, Roseland

Jersey Rain

The Passaic River laps our district's edges. The geographic record tracks its highs and lows, as do human-made rain gauges, flood maps, and insurance company documents. For four days, your group will review some of these records, test them, and tell the story of extreme rain in our neighborhood.

How It Works

  • We're working in groups of three or four. Each person will play a specific role.
  • Day 1: We read the river by pulling rainfall data and creating a chart.
  • Day 2: We observe the swings between droughts and floods. We'll also begin building our StoryMaps.
  • Day 3: We'll consider the deeper implications of the phenomenon. Who watches the river, creates the records, examines them for patterns? Who pays the price when the Passaic rises from its bed? We'll add a closing slide that connects our data to who pays.
  • Day 4: We follow the water from our school to Newark Bay. Then we hold a hearing: when the Passaic floods, who should hold the water, and who should pay to stop it?
  • Day 5 (optional): We share our StoryMaps in a gallery walk and question each other's work. (You know, the scientific method and all that!)
  • Your group will create one StoryMap with StoryMapJS (opens in a new tab), a free mapping tool from Northwestern University's Knight Lab. One person will sign in with their school Google account to build, but everybody must contribute.
  • Be sure to consult the Checklist before you share!

The River Remembers

Read the background

The Pine Brook gauge traces records back as far as 1903, and that first year was one of the worst. Only a year earlier, in 1902, the Passaic had flooded so badly that government scientists called it "the most disastrous flood ever known in the Passaic Valley" (which might explain why they felt the need to begin tracking the river). In October 1903, more than 10 inches of rain fell on the Passaic basin. The river crested at 23.2 feet and stayed out of its banks for 12 days.

The highest crest on record came in August 2011, when Hurricane Irene pushed the river to 24.12 feet.

In December 2023, the river flooded again. Fairfield declared a state of emergency, closed its schools, and shut roads including Bloomfield Avenue and Horseneck Road.

Of the 77 floods recorded at Pine Brook through 2019, 34 came in spring. What does this suggest about flooding? Is it perhaps seasonal?

Pull the Data | Hydrologist leads

Our closest official weather station is at Essex County Airport, which is in Fairfield (and outside our library windows). The National Weather Service calls it Caldwell, NJ.

  1. Open NOWData (opens in a new tab) from the National Weather Service.
  2. Under Location, choose Caldwell, NJ.
  3. Choose Monthly summarized data.
  4. Set the variable to Precipitation and the summary to Sum. Pick the last two full years. Click Go.
  5. In a second window, choose Daily/monthly normals for Caldwell, NJ. Pick Monthly and set the variable to Precipitation. These are the 1991 to 2020 averages.
  6. Copy the last 13 months of totals and the normal for each month into your group's Google Sheet.

Watch for the letter M. It means data are missing for that month. Write down every M you find.

Reading the Flood Map

Light blue: 1% Annual Chance Flood Hazard (Zone A or AE). FEMA's high-risk area, also called the Special Flood Hazard Area or the 100-year floodplain. In Zone AE, FEMA has calculated a Base Flood Elevation: the height the water is expected to reach in that flood.

Red diagonal stripes: Regulatory Floodway. The river channel and the land beside it that must stay open so flood water can pass. Building here can raise flood levels upstream.

Orange: 0.2% Annual Chance Flood Hazard (Zone X, shaded). Moderate risk; sometimes called the 500-year floodplain.

No color: Area of Minimal Flood Hazard (Zone X, unshaded). Higher than the 500-year flood. Minimal is not zero!

Map number and effective date: printed above the map. The number names the map panel you're reading; the date tells you how old it is.

Important Note: "100-year" doesn't mean one flood every hundred years! It means a 1% chance of flooding in any given year.

The Evidence Trail

Hydrologist and Archivist

  1. NOAA, a federal science agency, kept a database of every billion-dollar weather disaster in the United States from 1980 to 2024. It combined data from FEMA, insurance companies, and state agencies. In May 2025, NOAA retired it. A spokesperson explained that the change fit "evolving priorities, statutory mandates, and staffing changes."
  2. In October 2025, Climate Central, a nonprofit research group, brought the database back. It hired Adam Smith, the scientist who ran it at NOAA, and followed the same methods.
  3. In July 2025, the federal website for the National Climate Assessments went offline. These are reports Congress requires. As of this writing, copies remain in NOAA's online library.
  4. In late 2025, Zillow, the largest home listing site, removed climate risk scores from more than a million listings after a California listing service questioned the flood models. Zillow's own research had found that homes with high fire or flood scores were less likely to sell.

A note on method: NOAA's database never claimed to measure climate change alone. Costs also rise when more people build in harm's way.

Talk it over

  • Who's still keeping the evidence? Who stopped?
  • Who benefits when risk is harder to see?
  • How does the note on method change how you read the numbers?

The Money Trail

Cartographer and Analyst

  1. Between 1936 and 1966, the federal government spent more than 7 billion dollars on dams and levees, but flood losses kept rising. A task force led by the geographer Gilbert White told Congress to change course: map the danger, plan around it, and study flood insurance. President Lyndon Johnson (LYNDON! BAINES! JOHNSON! MY PROBLEMATIC FAVE!) sent the report to Congress with a warning: "Nature will always extract some price for use of her flood plains."
  2. In 1968, Congress created the National Flood Insurance Program. Communities that join must use flood maps and manage building in flood zones.
  3. For decades, many policies cost less than the real risk. In 2021, FEMA launched Risk Rating 2.0, the biggest change to how the program sets prices since the 1970s. Prices now depend on each home's features, so some owners pay more and some pay less.
  4. In 2023, after Tropical Storm Ida, New Jersey replaced the rainfall numbers in its building rules. The old numbers came from data that ended in 1999. New buildings in river flood zones must now sit two feet higher.

Talk it over

  • Who pays when a price is too low? Who pays when it is too high?
  • Why would an insurer care about the evidence trail?
  • What do flood maps have to do with your StoryMap?

Follow the Water

Read the background

Our school sits between Green Brook and Grandview. Green Brook flows into Deepavaal Brook, which drains the old Long Meadow, runs past Essex County Airport, and empties into the Passaic River in Fairfield.

The Passaic wraps around Fairfield's western and northern edges. At Two Bridges, the Pompton River flows in. Then the river narrows at Singac and drops over Beatties Dam at Little Falls on its way to Paterson and Newark Bay.

Here's the kicker: Little Falls is a bottleneck. In a big flood, water backs up behind it, and Fairfield's low, flat land contains that backlog. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers explains that this makes flooding worse upstream and reduces flood peaks downstream.

  1. Use the drawing to trace water from the area near our school to Newark Bay.
  2. Mark Green Brook, the airport, Two Bridges, Singac, and Little Falls on your county map.
  3. Answer as a group: Who benefits from the water Fairfield holds? Who pays for it?

The Old Fight | Archivist leads

People have fought over the dam at Little Falls for more than a quarter millennium. (Really!)

Document A. In 1772, Captain James Gray ran an iron foundry and mill at Little Falls, damming the river to power them. The dam flooded farms upstream. A history of Paterson published in 1920 says the legislature passed an act that year "to put a stop to the flooding of extensive areas of land above the dam" by lowering it. The Passaic County Historical Society shares a different account. In their version of events, the farmers tore the dam down and Gray asked the legislature to make them pay.

Document B. The same 1920 history says the legislature passed a nearly identical act in 1872. It concludes, "Legislatures come and go, but the dam seems to remain."

Document C. In 1858, the state created a commission to dig a drainage channel from Pine Brook to the mouth of Deepavaal Brook. Its records, now at the New Jersey Historical Society, include lists of the "owners and possessors of flowed lands on the Passaic."

Sources: Nelson & Shriner, 1920; New Jersey Historical Society, n.d.; Passaic County Historical Society, n.d.

Talk it over

  • The two accounts of 1772 disagree. What would you need to see to settle it?
  • What do you think "flowed lands" means? Who owned them?
  • Who had the power to change the river in 1772? Who has it now?

The Hearing | Whole class

On March 25, 2014, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers came to Fairfield to present its options for the Passaic. We'll hold our own hearing today.

Each group gets one question card. Each person speaks for one stakeholder: Fairfield, Little Falls and Singac, Paterson, or the Passaic River Coalition. In a group of three, leave out the Coalition.

A benefit-cost ratio compares the flood damage a plan would prevent to what it would cost. Above 1.0, the plan saves more than it costs. Below 1.0, it costs more than it saves. (Don't forget: that's just money!)

  1. Read your question card and your stakeholder card.
  2. On your own, decide: does this plan help or hurt the people you speak for? Find one fact on the cards that supports your answer.
  3. Argue it out as a group. Remember that every plan competes with doing nothing, which costs the basin more than $240 million a year in flood damage.
  4. Vote. In your group's shared Google Doc, write your decision and the strongest argument against it.
  5. Present your decision to the class in two minutes.

Question Cards | One per group

Your group's card:

Card 1

Should New Jersey rebuild Beatties Dam and build a new dam at Two Bridges?

The plan: Rebuild Beatties Dam at its current height, build a new 25-foot dam at Two Bridges, deepen and widen about 13 miles of river, and use Great Piece Meadows as a reservoir.

Cost: $1.9 billion. Benefit-cost ratio: 0.6 to 0.8.

History: The state asked the Corps to study this plan in 2011. Back in 1987, a Corps study recommended gates for Beatties Dam; the state set aside $5 million in 1988, then took it back in 1989.

Little Falls officials said in 2011 that gates would have "virtually no downstream impact." The Corps says the water held above Little Falls is what protects the towns below it.

Sources: Mancuso, 2011b; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, & New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, 2013, 2014

Card 2

Should New Jersey bring back the flood tunnel?

The plan: A 20-mile tunnel, 42 feet across, carries Pompton River floodwater under North Jersey to Newark Bay. Levees and floodwalls protect towns along the lower river.

Cost: $4.7 billion. Benefit-cost ratio: 1.02 to 1.44. It protects the whole basin against a 100-year flood.

History: Congress approved the tunnel in 1990. The state dropped it in 1996, and since 2000 federal law has barred funding.

According to the Corps, the tunnel "would increase flood risks downstream" if it weren't accompanied by levees. The Passaic River Coalition opposed it for its impact on trees, wildlife, and groundwater. In 2019, local officials called it "the only comprehensive option."

Sources: Hayes, 2024; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, n.d.-a, n.d.-b; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, & New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, 2013, 2014

Card 3

Should the state flood-proof, raise, and buy homes instead of building anything big?

The plan: Flood-proof about 8,700 buildings, raise about 650 homes, put walls around about 500 more, and buy out 68.

Cost: $1.2 billion. Benefit-cost ratio: 1.3 to 1.92, the best on the Corps' list, though the Corps warned these benefits may be overstated.

Buyouts are voluntary; the land acquired would become open space.

"Buyouts don't stop the water," a Fairfield resident said in 2011. In Paterson, about one in four homes is owner-occupied.

Sources: Mancuso, 2011a; Passaic River Basin Flood Advisory Commission, 2011; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, & New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, 2014; U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.-c

Card 4

Should towns protect themselves with levees and floodwalls?

The plan: Build 24 miles of levees, 17 miles of floodwalls, and 46 pump stations, plus about 4,300 smaller fixes to individual buildings.

Cost: $3.1 billion. Benefit-cost ratio: 0.8 to 1.2.

The Passaic River Coalition warns that levees can be overtopped if a storm exceeds their design parameters. When Congress approved the 1990 plan, it removed Bergen County's levees and required proof that the project wouldn't worsen flooding in Bergen County.

If your town builds a wall, where does the water go?

Sources: Passaic River Coalition, n.d.; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, & New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, 2013, 2014

Card 5

Should Fairfield's meadows stay wild so the river has somewhere to go?

The plan: Buy and protect the wetlands that hold floodwater. The Corps planned to preserve 5,350 acres and had bought about 3,400 by 2013. State law already forbids reducing flood storage in the basin.

Cost: Not priced as a separate plan.

The Corps says water held in the basin lowers flood peaks for the towns below Little Falls. In 2011, a Fairfield citizens' group called for deepening the river and adding floodgates at Little Falls instead.

Is it fair to ask one town's land to hold everyone's water? Who should pay for it?

Sources: Liang, n.d.; Mancuso, 2011a; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, & New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, 2013

Card 6

Should towns clear fallen trees and sandbars from the river?

The plan: Remove fallen trees and built-up sediment that slow the water. The governor's flood commission recommended making this routine in 2011. In 2025, a group of upriver towns led by Berkeley Heights began seeking funding to do it.

Cost: Not priced by the Corps.

"Doing nothing is not the solution," Berkeley Heights Mayor Angie Devanney said. The Corps sees less payoff: dredging without reshaping the channel "would have a limited impact."

Sources: Passaic River Basin Flood Advisory Commission, 2011; Township of Berkeley Heights, 2025; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, New York District, n.d.-b
